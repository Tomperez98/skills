# Picking a good idea

You don't have an idea yet, or the ones you have don't excite you. The goal
is one idea, stated in a sentence, specific enough to spec next. Apply the
rules in order.

## 1. Start from a trend

A good idea is typically tied to a trend — something already moving that
carries the product forward instead of you pushing it uphill. Start by
listing trends you can actually observe: a tool everyone is adopting, a
behavior that is shifting, a platform that just opened up.

If the trend is something you *feel* rather than something you can show, hand
the question to a deep-research skill before you spec — it returns a cited brief
naming the trend, the incumbents, and the gap, per
[RESEARCH.md](RESEARCH.md) rule 1.

## 2. State the idea as one sentence

Once a trend suggests an idea, write it as one sentence: *for whom, doing
what, with what result.* A sentence forces you to name the user and the
outcome instead of gesturing at a category. "An app" is not an idea; "a
camera app that maps a face and tells you exactly what to fix" is.

```text
Idea:   For <who>, <doing what>, so that <result>.
Trend:  <what is moving, and where you can show it>
```

## 3. Prefer the shortest path to value

Between two ideas, pick the one a user can feel the payoff from fastest.
The five-minute rule applies from the very first choice: an idea whose value
takes an hour to reach is the wrong idea, however clever.

## 4. One idea at a time

Pick one, spec it, and validate it before reaching for the next; park the
rest.

## Done when

- The trend is one you can show, or a cited research brief backs it
  (rule 1).
- The idea is written as one sentence naming who, doing what, with what
  result (rule 2).
- You picked one idea, the one with the fastest payoff, and the rest are
  parked (rules 3–4).
